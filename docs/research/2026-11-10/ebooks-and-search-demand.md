## 0. Read this first: what the data is and isn't

- **Amazon ranks, ratings and review counts** were pulled live on 10 Nov 2026 (Amazon US product pages via Firecrawl's Amazon provider; one Amazon UK check). Ranks move hourly, so treat them as a snapshot.
- **"Most downloaded" isn't public for gated guides.** No platform publishes download counts. Gated guides below are listed because they rank for the topic and are the vendors' flagship lead magnets, not because of download evidence.
- **Goodreads and Google Books:** the Goodreads search only returned study-guide spin-offs, not the main editions, so no Goodreads ratings were captured. Google Books wasn't checked. Amazon review counts are the evidence used.
- **Search data is impressions and average position from Google Search Console (via Rank Math on convenexevents.com), not search volume.** We have no volume or keyword-difficulty figures. DataForSEO wasn't used (out of credit).
- **The Rank Math 12-month and 3-month keyword lists are capped at 100 rows.** Zero-click rows come back in alphabetical order, so both lists stop at queries starting "e" to "g". The 30-day list is complete. If a topic (event ROI, for example) doesn't appear, that's backed up by the complete 30-day list, but it's worth a full export from GSC to confirm.
- **People Also Ask (PAA), related searches and autocomplete** came from Apify (Google Search Scraper, UK, English; Google Autocomplete Scraper, GB, with question prefixes). These show what people type, not how often.
- **No copying.** Every item below is a market signal. Any Convene X guide gets written from our own experience and point of view. We don't reuse anyone's structure, wording, frameworks or examples.

### Core promise, what readers say, and the gap for each

**1. The Art of Gathering**
- *Core promise:* Meetings, dinners and conferences fail because hosts don't decide what they're for. Purpose first, then guest list, then format.
- *Readers praise (Amazon "Customers say"):* insightful, practical, "a blueprint for planning", easy to read, turns the mundane into the meaningful.
- *Readers complain:* too long, repetitive, "could have been shorter"; a minority call it self-promotional.
- *Gap:* It's about gatherings in general (weddings, birthdays, conferences). There's nothing on a sponsor-hosted business dinner where the host has a commercial interest and the guests can smell a pitch. A Convene X guide can take "decide what the evening is for" and apply it to the commercial reality: who signs off the guest list, why the host doesn't present, how Chatham House works at a table of 12.

**2. Setting the Table**
- *Core promise:* Hospitality, how people feel they're treated, is the business advantage.
- *Praise:* lessons on every page, applies beyond restaurants, great storytelling.
- *Complaints:* rambles, repetitive in the second half.
- *Gap:* A restaurateur's memoir, not a how-to. Business readers love the philosophy but have to translate it themselves. A guide can do that translation for one setting: the private dining room with 12 senior guests and a sponsor.

**3. Never Eat Alone**
- *Core promise:* Build relationships deliberately, including by hosting dinners.
- *Praise:* readable, specific, actionable relationship advice.
- *Complaints:* repetitive; self-promotional, boastful tone.
- *Gap:* Written for the individual networker, not for a company hosting a room. It shows that "dinner as relationship builder" sells, but the reader who's paying for the evening has no playbook.

**4. Event Planning: Management & Marketing (Genadinik)**
- *Core promise:* Run an event series that generates revenue or leads.
- *Reviews:* rating only, no review text captured.
- *Gap:* 74 pages aimed at people starting events as a business. Nothing on senior audiences or invitation-only formats.

**5. Event Planning: The Ultimate Guide (Judy Allen)**
- *Core promise:* An A to Z reference covering every event type from galas to conventions.
- *Praise:* comprehensive, authoritative, good checklists for small details.
- *Complaints:* hard to read; layout (readers wanted a lay-flat, workbook-style format); mixed views on value.
- *Gap:* 2008 edition, so no virtual, no LinkedIn-era invitation, no GDPR. Broad rather than deep. Readers' format complaint is useful: they want something they can work from, not read cover to cover.

**6. The Event Manager's Bible (Conway, UK)**
- *Core promise:* A detailed operational manual: planning, health and safety, budgets.
- *Reviews:* rating only (4.5, 138); no review text on the page.
- *Gap:* Operations-heavy and UK-relevant, but nothing on recruiting senior delegates or protecting a room from sales behaviour.

**7. Meeting Design (Hoffman)**
- *Core promise:* Design meetings as you'd design a product.
- *Reviews:* rating only.
- *Gap:* Internal team meetings. The facilitation thinking transfers to roundtables, but nobody's applied it to external senior-peer discussions.

**8. Producing Virtual Training, Meetings, and Webinars (LaBorie)**
- *Core promise:* Master the producer role and the technology behind virtual sessions.
- *Reviews:* rating only.
- *Gap:* Training and L&D focus. Nothing on getting Directors to turn up, or on 6 to 12-person virtual roundtables.

**9. Reinventing Virtual Events (Michael, Nimchinski)**
- *Core promise:* Turn low-attendance "ghost webinars" into interactive go-to-market events.
- *Reviews:* 4.9 from 34 ratings; no review text captured.
- *Gap:* Sales-led and US-centric. The problem it names (empty webinars) is real, which tells us "how do I get senior people to show up" resonates.

**10. Transitioning to Virtual and Hybrid Events (Chodor, Cyranski)**
- *Core promise:* Move live events online and market them.
- *Reviews:* rating only (4.0).
- *Gap:* Written in 2020 for the pandemic pivot to large virtual events. Dated, and not about small senior formats.

**11. Proving the Value of Meetings and Events (Phillips)**
- *Core promise:* The ROI Methodology applied to meetings: measure from reaction through to financial return.
- *Reviews:* none on Amazon; out of print, $99.99 third-party.
- *Gap:* The serious ROI method is hard to get hold of and built for large corporate meetings. Small-room event ROI has no accessible, practical guide.

**12. Cvent's Essential Guide to Event ROI (gated)**
- *Contents (from the landing page):* Defining ROI and what's different for events; 8 drivers of event ROI; metrics, KPIs and maths; setting goals and expectations; measuring, reporting and analysing.
- *Gap:* Platform-centric and built for conference-scale programmes (registrations, attendance, app engagement). Nothing on measuring a 12-person dinner where the value is 2 or 3 relationships.

**13. Bizzabo: Maximizing Event ROI (gated)**
- *Promise:* A framework to measure and showcase impact; 25+ metrics with examples; justify budgets.
- *Gap:* Same as Cvent: metric volume suited to big events. 25 metrics is the opposite of what a small-room host needs.

**14. ON24 2026 Digital Engagement Benchmarks (gated)**
- *Promise:* Benchmarks for webinar attendance, engagement and conversion across thousands of customers.
- *Gap:* Benchmarks describe mass webinars (a 2026 social post citing the report puts the average at 239 attendees per webinar; [CONFIRM THIS] against the report before any reuse). No benchmark exists for invitation-only senior webinars of 25 to 50 registrations.

**15. Dinner-Led Growth (ungated)**
- *Promise:* 4 frameworks for structuring an evening executives want to attend, strong opinions, "trade secrets", 60 pages.
- *Gap:* One US practitioner's view, built to sell their own dinner series. Little sign of the guest-list sign-off, seniority gating or UK context.

### The pattern across all 15

1. **The best-selling books are about hosting and relationships in general** (Parker, Meyer, Ferrazzi: 2,300 to 3,830 ratings each). The event-planning manuals sell far less (under 350 ratings each) and readers call them long, dense or dated.
2. **Nobody owns "the sponsor-hosted executive dinner".** The only dedicated pieces are a free US blog post and one consultant's e-book. Neither is written from the senior guest's side.
3. **ROI guides are built for big events and platforms.** No one has written "how to measure a small room honestly".
4. **Virtual books are pandemic-era or training-focused.** Small virtual roundtables (6 to 12 people) have no guide at all.
5. **Readers keep saying "too long, repetitive".** A short, practical, workbook-style guide is a format advantage in itself.

## 3. Lead-magnet e-book ideas

Titles contain no personal names. Proof points in any of these must use the approved framing only ("our team has run 34 events in the last 18 months, 32 of them at or over capacity, across their careers / in previous roles"). No promised leads, meetings or ROI.

### Idea 1 (recommended lead): The Senior-Leader Dinner Playbook: how to host an executive dinner Directors stay for

**For:** would-be hosts (B2B marketing and sales leaders).

**Outline**
1. Decide what the evening is for: one conversation, one theme, no presentation
2. The 12-week clock: about 2 weeks prep, 10 weeks recruitment, and what happens when you rush it
3. Director+ means Director+: building the guest list against your ideal customer, and why the host signs it off
4. The invitation that gets a yes: what senior people need to know before they give up an evening (with fill-in templates)
5. 4 dinner formats for 10 to 25 guests, and when to use each
6. Running the table: Chatham House Rule, the host's role, the moment a pitch loses the room
7. Venue and table plan: private room checklist (draws on the site's existing London venue pages)
8. The morning after: follow-up that continues the conversation instead of selling
9. Measuring it honestly: what to track for a room of 12 (bridges to Idea 3)
10. One-page checklist and a 12-week planner

**Why it beats what's out there**
- The best-sellers (hosting philosophy, 2,300 to 3,830 ratings each) prove the appetite but don't cover a sponsor-hosted business dinner. The only dedicated guides are a US blog post focused on lead capture and one US consultant's e-book.
- No one writes from the senior guest's side. Our wedge, "the best sponsored event is the one where nobody sells", is a real point of difference, not a rephrase.
- UK-specific, short and workbook-shaped, which answers the most common complaint about the manuals (too long, hard to work from).
- Backed by the site's biggest topic cluster (executive dinner queries: 400+ impressions on the head term, 116 on formats) and by PAA/autocomplete demand for definitions, invitations and dress code.

### Idea 2: Roundtable, Dinner or Webinar? Choosing the right room to reach senior buyers

**For:** hosts deciding on a format and budget.

**Outline**
1. 4 formats, side by side: executive dinner (10 to 25), virtual roundtable (6 to 12), senior webinar (25 to 50 registrations), 1-2-1 meetings
2. Roundtable vs panel vs webinar: what each does to the conversation
3. Which seniority each format attracts, and how to gate it
4. Running a virtual roundtable people speak at: the host, the moderator, the camera-on question
5. Senior webinars: why 40 of the right people beats 400 of the wrong ones, and how long it should run
6. Decision matrix: goal, audience, budget, timeline, then format
7. Combining formats into a short series

**Why it beats what's out there**
- The virtual-event books are pandemic-era (2020 to 2022) or written for training teams. None covers small senior virtual roundtables.
- Vendor guides compare platforms, not formats. Ours is format-neutral and objective, which is the kind of comparison content AI assistants tend to cite.
- Backed by the strongest roundtable data we have (331 impressions on "b2b virtual roundtable" at position 9, 42 on "hosting b2b roundtables" in 30 days), and builds on existing pages (/how-to-host-a-virtual-roundtable/, /executive-webinars-vs-virtual-roundtables/, /b2b-executive-webinar/).

### Idea 3: Measuring a Small Room: an honest guide to the value of executive dinners and roundtables

**For:** hosts who need to justify the budget internally.

**Outline**
1. Why conference ROI maths breaks for a room of 12
2. What to agree before the event: the 3 or 4 outcomes worth tracking
3. Guest-list quality as the first measure: did the right people come?
4. Conversation signals: what to note on the night, and what not to
5. The 90 days after: relationship progress, without inventing attribution
6. Sponsorship value when there's no badge scan
7. A one-page scorecard template (plus a simple worksheet)
8. How to report it to a CFO without overclaiming

**Why it beats what's out there**
- Cvent and Bizzabo own "event ROI" with conference-scale guides (Bizzabo lists 25+ metrics). The one serious methodology book is out of print at $99.99. Nobody has written for the small room.
- Autocomplete shows people want a template, calculator, formula and "what is a good ROI". A scorecard template is a natural download.
- The site currently has **zero** impressions for event ROI queries, so this opens a new cluster rather than competing with our own pages.
- Fits the brand rule: it teaches how to measure, it doesn't promise returns.

**Recommendation:** Lead with Idea 1. It's where our own search data, the market gap and the brand's wedge line up. Idea 3 is the strongest second asset (new cluster, clear template demand). Idea 2 could be a shorter guide or a section of Idea 1.
