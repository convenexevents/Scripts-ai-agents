Before a senior leader gives up an evening, they check 4 things. The menu isn't one of them.

1. Who else is coming. By role and seniority, not by name. A room of peers is the reason to say yes.

Our team learned this in previous roles, writing invitations for Directors and C-level guests. It's why every invitation we send, to guests across the UK, Europe and the US, starts from the guest's side of the table.

## Tuesday 17 Nov: The host who doesn't pitch

**Reader:** would-be hosts. **Format:** text only. **Goal:** comments (the argument post of the week).
**Posting time:** company page 11:00; personal 15:00 (slot B).

### Company post (201 words)

```text
The best sponsored dinner is the one where the sponsor doesn't pitch.

Buyers still want people in the conversation. What they avoid is being sold to.

Gartner found 67% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience (Gartner, 9 Mar 2026). In a separate survey, 69% said they turn to sales reps to validate AI-generated insights (Gartner, 20 May 2026).

Read together, buyers still want expertise. They want it without the pitch. A table of peers gives them that, as long as the host lets it.

The moment it goes wrong is usually halfway through the main course. The conversation turns to a problem the host's product solves, and the host leans in.

That's the moment to stay quiet.

What a good host does instead:
Opens with the table question, then hands it over.
Introduces guests to each other, not to the product.
Speaks last.
Follows up afterwards with something useful, not a deck.

The relationship comes from the quality of the evening, not from airtime. Guests remember who gave them a good conversation. They also remember who turned it into a demo.

If you've hosted one of these, what was the hardest part of staying quiet?

Link in the first comment.
```
