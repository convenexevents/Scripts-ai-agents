```
A conference gives you reach. A small room gives you depth.

Both have a job. Conferences put you in front of a crowd. A small room puts you at a table with a handful of senior people, one question and no pitch.

In a hall, people collect cards and move on. At a table of 10, everyone hears everyone, and nobody hides behind a lanyard.

3 things change in a small room:

1. Everyone gets to speak, so the quiet voices get heard.
2. The question on the invitation shapes the whole evening.
3. Peers compare notes instead of trading pitches.

It's about connecting with peers, not selling to them.

What's the best room you've been in, and what made it work?
```

What changed from the signed-off text: the 69% said "turn to sales reps to validate", and Gartner says "prefer to validate". The two figures were called "a separate survey", which is wrong. Per Felix, the post no longer says whether they are one survey ("the same buyer research" was our inference, not Gartner's statement). It states only that fieldwork for both ran in Aug to Sep 2025. Our reading is now labelled as ours and appears once. Both Gartner release URLs go in the first comment as sources.

```
The best sponsored dinner is the one where the sponsor doesn't pitch.

Gartner found 67% of B2B buyers prefer a rep-free experience (Gartner, 9 Mar 2026). Gartner also found 69% prefer to validate AI-generated insights with sales reps (Gartner, 20 May 2026). Fieldwork for both ran in Aug to Sep 2025.

Our reading: buyers still want people in the conversation. What they avoid is being sold to. A table of peers gives them that, as long as the host lets it.

The moment it goes wrong is usually halfway through the main course. The conversation turns to a problem the host's product solves, and the host leans in.

That's the moment to stay quiet.

What a good host does instead:

1. Opens with the table question, then hands it over.
2. Introduces guests to each other, not to the product.
3. Speaks last.
4. Follows up afterwards with something useful, not a deck.

The relationship comes from the quality of the evening, not from airtime. Guests remember who gave them a good conversation. They also remember who turned it into a demo.

If you've hosted one of these, what was the hardest part of staying quiet?

Link in the first comment.
```
